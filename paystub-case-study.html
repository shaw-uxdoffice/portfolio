01 — Context & Background

A compliance-critical
gap in enterprise payroll

Lyric is ADP's enterprise payroll platform. Two types of practitioners rely on it daily — COS teams managing payroll on behalf of clients, and non-COS HR administrators running their own. When either group needs pay stubs for legal, audit, or offboarding purposes, the stakes are immediate and the margin for error is zero.


The feature needed to achieve parity with Vantage and Enterprise products, which already supported bulk download. The gap wasn't just a UX inconvenience — it was leaving COS teams exposed to weeks of manual labor for every legal or DOL audit request.

02 — The Problem to Solve

One by one, all week long

When a client received a DOL audit notice or an employee filed a lawsuit, a COS practitioner would open iReports, search through a PDF that could contain 25,000 employees, extract individual pages, and repeat — for every pay period across years of history. Lyric improved the interface but not the loop.


And the compliance pressure was real. Incorrect pay statements don't just create operational delays — in states like New York, they create legal exposure. A wrong statement can end in a lawsuit. That's the environment in which this feature needed to work flawlessly.

The Core Problem

— Nitin Sharma, Manager CSMPS, COS

04 — Discovery & Research

Three findings that changed the design

The Design Thinking workshop ran for 90 minutes with five participants across compliance, COS operations, and product. The most important insights weren't in the initial problem statement — they emerged from the debate.


Finding 01 — Two Distinct Mental Models
Users come to download documents or to verify totals. These are separate cognitive tasks. Designing a single unified interface for both created confusion, not convenience. This finding drove the eventual separation of Quick Totals from the bulk download flow.
Finding 02 — Payroll Results ≠ Pay Statements
Payroll Results are internal — used for reconciliation, available in Excel. Pay Statements are legally binding, external-only, always PDF, governed by state compliance rules and produced via the iPay API. Mixing them in a single interface wasn't just confusing — it was a compliance risk.
Finding 03 — iPay API Was a Hard Constraint
The iPay system never supported bulk operations. Each statement request goes through AVOD authentication individually. Any bulk solution would require sequential API calls, meaning the UX needed to handle graceful loading states rather than instant delivery.
"Just imagine the interface on your Android or Apple — when you share a picture, you see all the options at once. Bluetooth, hotspot, all of it. Why do I have to toggle through multiple screens when the use case is the same?"

05 — Three Concepts Explored

The right answer required the wrong ones first

The workshop generated three distinct design directions. Each was sketched, critiqued in group session, and ultimately tested against real users. The process of elimination was as important as the final direction.

My initial hypothesis — that a unified modal combining bulk downloads, quick totals, and detailed breakdowns would serve users best — failed at round one of testing. Users were confused by mixing downloading with data viewing. The pivot came from listening to that confusion rather than defending the design.

Concept A
Toolbar with Modal
Discarded
Gmail-style toolbar with action buttons. Multi-select opens a modal combining Quick Totals, download format, and pay statement generation in tabs.
Testing showed confusion: "Is this for downloading or just viewing data?" Nested modals for pay statements added friction rather than removing it.
Concept B
Three Segmented Buttons
Considered
Three independent action buttons — Pay Statement, Pay Summary, Quick Totals — each launching its own modal. Clearest separation of use cases, fastest path per task.
Button proliferation risk: as feature scope grows with future tax statements and W-4s, this pattern breaks down. Deferred, not abandoned.
Concept C
Right Panel with Live Updates
Preferred Direction
Split-screen: left panel for filtering and selection, right panel updating totals in real time. Single Download CTA with conditional configuration based on Internal vs. External selection.
Best for error prevention — "preview before commit" aligned with Lyric's core design pattern. Required Waypoint design system investigation for right-panel component.

06 — The Final Solution

Separate the tasks. Simplify each one.

After user testing revealed that combining bulk downloads and quick totals into a single flow created confusion, the architecture shifted. Two separate, focused experiences — each optimised for its distinct use case — turned out to work better than any unified approach.

The key design principle wasn't "put everything in one place." It was "make the 80% use case invisible." For bulk downloads: filter, select all, download. Three actions, one CTA. For Quick Totals: a focused modal showing aggregate figures from selected periods, with an optional export — entirely separate from the download flow.

Multi-Select Table with Contextual CTA
Checkbox selection with "Select All" for the filtered view. The action button label updates in real time: "Download 12 Pay Stubs" rather than a static "Download." No ambiguity about what you're about to commit to.
02
Quick Totals as a Separate Modal
Gross, Net, Taxes, Deductions aggregated from selected pay periods. A dedicated, non-blocking experience. Answering "what's the total?" does not require generating or downloading a single document.
03
"Exclude $0 Paychecks" Toggle
The one configuration that genuinely matters. Legal and DOL requests don't always need zero-pay periods. This toggle eliminates irrelevant records before selection, not after. Smart default > optional setting.
04
Progress Indicator for Large Batches
iPay's API processes statements sequentially, not in parallel. The design communicates that honestly: a progress state for batches over 50, with a warning for very large requests rather than a silent timeout.

07 — Challenges & Concepts Not Pursued

Honest about what didn't work

Three of the most important design decisions in this project were decisions not to build something. Each discarded direction taught something that improved the final solution.

The most instructive failure was the unified modal — my original hypothesis. User testing was direct: mixing downloading with data viewing confused people. They came with a task in mind. An interface that made them choose between two different tasks before executing either was friction, not consolidation.

Concept Discarded
Unified Tabbed Modal

The initial concept bundled bulk downloads, Quick Totals, and detailed breakdown into a single modal with tabs — plus configuration options for SSN masking and YTD toggles. Round one user testing was unambiguous: "Is this for downloading or just viewing data?" Configuration options felt overwhelming for what users described as a quick task. We separated the use cases entirely and removed redundant controls.

Deferred — Not Abandoned
Unified "Payroll Results + Pay Statements" Table

The majority of workshop participants wanted the two tabs merged into one. The technical reality — different data sources, different legal treatment, and upcoming tax statements requirements — made this premature for MVP. The two-tab approach was kept with a documented roadmap for consolidation pending engineering assessment.

Technical Constraint
Native Bulk Download via iPay API

The iPay API was built for individual statement retrieval. Each request requires separate AVOD authentication. The "15-minute automated solution" from the workshop would require API negotiation with the iPay team and GSO involvement. MVP used sequential calls with a progress indicator — an honest and reliable fallback that communicated the delay rather than hiding it.

Deprioritized
Mobile-Optimised Experience

Bulk selection and multi-column compliance tables don't translate cleanly to mobile. Platform analytics confirmed that HR administrators and COS practitioners almost exclusively use desktop. A basic mobile layout was designed, but the mobile-first experience was explicitly scoped to a future release. Naming the trade-off clearly prevented it from becoming a gap that surprised anyone.

08 — Impact & Outcomes

From a week of manual work to under two minutes

The metrics were not aspirational targets set at the beginning of the project. They were post-launch measurements taken after two weeks of live use. The numbers validated that separating use cases had been the right call.

The most meaningful outcome wasn't the time reduction, though 87.5% is significant. It was the 92% retention rate — practitioners who tried the feature came back. That number reflects something harder to design: confidence. The flow was clear enough that users trusted it the second time without hesitation.

09 — Reflections & Learnings

What this project changed about how I design

01

Separate Does Not Mean Fragmented

My starting assumption was that consolidation equals clarity — put everything in one place and users won't have to think. Testing disproved it. Users with a download task in mind were slowed down, not helped, by a modal that also offered totals and configuration. When two use cases have different enough intents, a single interface serves neither well. Focused, single-purpose tools can be more usable than feature-rich ones — and recognising that difference early is a core design skill.

02

Configuration Is Often a Signal of Unclear Requirements

The original unified modal had toggles for SSN masking, YTD options, and format selection. Most of these were redundant — the system already enforced SSN masking per organisational policy, and pay statements are always PDF by legal requirement. Configuration that duplicates existing system behaviour adds cognitive load without adding value. When you find yourself adding a lot of toggles and settings, ask first: could smart defaults make these unnecessary?

03

Test the Simplest Concept First

I tested the complex unified modal in round one because I assumed simpler flows needed less validation. They don't — they need it more, because the design has fewer places to hide when something's wrong. Testing a simple multi-select → download flow first would have established a baseline faster and led to the pivot earlier. The extra iteration time wasn't wasted — it built conviction — but that conviction could have come sooner.

04

Simplicity Is the Output of Discipline, Not Shortcuts

The final design — multi-select table, one CTA, one separate modal — looks obvious in hindsight. It was not obvious in process. It required testing three concepts, discarding one, deferring another, negotiating a technical constraint with engineering, and making the explicit call to separate two features that practitioners conflated. Simple interfaces are the result of many difficult decisions, not the absence of them.
